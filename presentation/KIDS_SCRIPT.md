# Our script — what we say

Three speakers. Swap however you like — nobody has to be "the clever one", the
slides carry you. Print one copy per person and highlight your own lines.

**Total: about 6 minutes.** That is the right length. Finish while they still
want more.

---

## Before you start (adult, 10 minutes of practice pays for itself)

- [ ] Practise 3 times out loud. Out loud is different from reading in your head.
- [ ] Say the hard words together until they feel normal: **col-LIDE-er**, **HAY-dron**, **KWARK**, **new-TREE-no**, **pro-TON**.
- [ ] Fire the model once before the audience comes in, and clear the beads back in.
- [ ] Open `simulation/index.html` in the browser *before* you start, so it is ready behind the slides.
- [ ] Nobody reads from the paper on the day. If you forget a line, just point at the picture — the picture is the talk.
- [ ] It is fine to say "I'll come back to that". It is not fine to make something up. Scientists say "I don't know" all the time.

## Slide 1 — Title (Everyone)

> **ALL:** Good morning! We are going to tell you about the biggest machine
> human beings have ever built — and show you the small one we made ourselves.
>
> **KID 1:** It is called a collider. It is 27 kilometres long.
>
> **KID 2:** It is colder than space.
>
> **KID 3:** And it smashes tiny particles together to find out what
> *everything* is made of.

---

## Slide 2 — Everything is made of tiny dots (Kid 1)

> Everything is made of atoms. Atoms are so small that there are more atoms in
> one of your fingernails than there are people on Earth.
>
> Inside an atom is a nucleus. Inside the nucleus are protons. And inside a
> proton there are even smaller dots called **quarks**.
>
> So here is our question. If a proton is too small to see — how did anyone
> find out what is inside it?

*(Let that question hang for two seconds. Do not answer it yet.)*

---

## Slide 3 — Smash it (Kid 1)

> You smash it!
>
> If you want to find out what is inside a piñata, you whack it and look at what
> falls out. Scientists do the same thing with protons. They smash two of them
> together really, really hard — and look at what flies out.

---

## Slide 4 — A racetrack for particles (Kid 1)

> A collider is a racetrack.
>
> Take a proton. Speed it up. Then speed it up again. And again. Magnets steer
> it round and round a giant ring, and a second beam goes the **opposite** way.
>
> And then — CRASH.

**Cue for the adult: fire the model now if you are doing the model before the
slides end. Otherwise go straight on.**

---

## Slide 5 — Magnets are the steering wheel (Kid 2)

> A proton going straight will never turn a corner. So around the ring there are
> thousands of giant electromagnets whose only job is to push the beam round the
> bend.
>
> And here is the tricky bit: the faster the protons go, the stronger the magnets
> have to be. In our computer simulation you can turn the magnets down and watch
> the beam fly straight off the ring. That really happens — it is called beam
> loss, and it is the one thing accelerator scientists worry about most.

---

## Slide 6 — RF cavities are the push (Kid 2)

> Going round and round is no good if you slow down.
>
> So there are boxes in the ring called RF cavities. They use radio waves to
> give the protons a push every single time they go past. It is like surfing a
> wave — every lap, another push.
>
> In our model, the electromagnets at the top are our version: they hold the
> balls, and when we press the button, that is the kick.

---

## Slide 7 — The real thing (Kid 2)

> The Large Hadron Collider is near Geneva, in Switzerland and France. It is
> buried about 100 metres under the ground.
>
> It is 27 kilometres round. The magnets are cooled to minus 271 degrees —
> colder than space. The protons go round 11,000 times every second, at
> 99.9999991 per cent of the speed of light.
>
> Say that number out loud. Nine nines. And it is still not quite the fastest
> anything can go.

---

## Slide 8 — E = mc² (Kid 2)

> When two protons crash, something amazing happens. Einstein worked out that
> **energy can turn into matter**.
>
> So the crash makes brand new particles that were not there before. The
> detector is a giant camera that catches them. That is the whole point of the
> machine — the crash is not the accident. The crash *is* the experiment.

---

## Slide 9 — Our model (Kid 3)

> So we built one.
>
> Two clear tubes. Two steel balls, and the balls are our protons. They are held
> at the top by two electromagnets we wound ourselves — 200 turns of wire each.
>
> When we press one button, both electromagnets let go at exactly the same
> moment, both balls race down in opposite directions, and they meet in the cup
> in the middle. CLACK. The beads that fly out are our new particles, and the
> buzzer is our detector going off.

**CUE: fire it. Let the beads fly. Do not talk over the crash — the pause is
the best bit. Then reload the beads calmly, it takes four seconds.**

---

## Slide 10 — What each part stands for (Kid 3)

> Everything on our board stands for a real thing. Read down the list with me.

*(Point at each one. Do not read it all yourself — ask the audience to call out
the next one. If they are quiet, just carry on.)*

---

## Slide 11 — Now the computer (Kid 3)

> We also built a computer simulation, because on a computer we can do things
> our model cannot — like showing the energy climbing, and showing what happens
> if the magnets are not strong enough.
>
> We need someone to press the big orange button. Who wants to?

**CUE: hand the mouse over. Turn the magnet slider down until protons fly off
the ring. Then turn it back up and press COLLIDE NOW. The audience will shout —
that is the point.**

---

## Slide 12 — Three things that blew our minds (Kid 1)

> Three things.
>
> One: the two beams pass through each other 11,000 times a second, but only
> about 20 protons in a billion actually crash. So the machine has to be
> enormous just to make the crashes happen.
>
> Two: the magnets have to be colder than space. If they warm up even a little,
> they stop working.
>
> Three: some particles — neutrinos — fly straight out of the detector and
> straight through the Earth without being seen at all. Most of what the crash
> makes, we never catch.

---

## Slide 13 — What we learned (Everyone — one line each)

> **KID 1:** We learned that tiny things can be smashed to reveal even tinier things.
>
> **KID 2:** We learned that magnets can steer something you cannot see.
>
> **KID 3:** And we learned that energy can turn into matter. Really.

---

## Slide 14 — Thank you (Everyone)

> **ALL:** Any questions? Come and press the button.

---

## Questions you will get — and good answers

**"Where is it?"** Buried under the ground near Geneva, on the border of
Switzerland and France, at a place called CERN.

**"Could it make a black hole?"** No. Space is hitting the Earth with particles
far more energetic than that, all the time, and has been for billions of years.
Nothing happened to us.

**"Is it dangerous?"** Only if you stood in the tunnel while it was running —
and nobody is allowed in there when it is on. The scientists stand in a control
room, sometimes in another country.

**"What happens to the protons afterwards?"** If the beam is not needed any more
it is steered into a big block of graphite at the end of the ring, which soaks
it up. They call it the beam dump.

**"How much does it cost?"** Billions. Lots of countries share the bill, which
is why thousands of scientists from all over the world work there together.

**"Did they find anything?"** Yes — in 2012 they found a brand new particle
called the Higgs boson, which people had predicted 48 years earlier. It won a
Nobel Prize.

**"Why is it so cold?"** The magnets are made of a special material that carries
electricity with no resistance at all — but only if it is freezing. Cold wires
can carry a much bigger push.

**"Can I see it?"** Parts of it. CERN runs free visits, and the detectors are
enormous — one of them is as big as a cathedral and weighs as much as the
Eiffel Tower.

**Something you cannot answer:** *"That is a brilliant question. We don't know —
but here is how we would find out."* That is the most scientific sentence in
this whole project.
